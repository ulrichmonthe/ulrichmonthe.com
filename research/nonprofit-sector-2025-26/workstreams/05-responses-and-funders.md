## Key findings

1. **"The Great Handoff" is not a sector term. It traces to a single vendor's marketing page.** Two targeted searches surfaced exactly one user: FundRobin, a grant-discovery vendor, in a "thought leadership" playbook. No foundation, association, academic center, or trade publication used it. **This is the client's coinage or the vendor's — it should not be presented to readers as established sector vocabulary.** The underlying *phenomenon* (federal→state pass-through devolution) is real and separately documented; the label is not.

2. **The single most important finding is a grantee-side contradiction of the funder-generosity narrative.** CEP's *State of Nonprofits 2026* reportedly finds close to **60% of nonprofit CEOs say foundation grants became *harder* to secure since January 2025**, and **more than 40% report actual reductions in foundation funding received**. Set against the funder-side story of surging payout and flexible dollars, this is the disconfirmation the brief asked me to hunt for — and it is the strongest card in this workstream. The money moved for a visible minority of grantees; the median grantee felt tightening.

3. **The payout-increase story is a small number of loud institutions against a flat sector aggregate.** Real, named, large commitments exist (MacArthur, Woods Fund, RWJF, McKnight, Northwest Area, TransitCenter). But independent-foundation payout for FYE2025 reportedly **did not meaningfully change and remained at 5%**, and CEP reportedly found **~70% of foundations had not increased payout** despite 87% seeing higher demand. The vanguard is genuine; the sector did not follow.

4. **The Level Up Pledge is the highest-value unevaluated commitment in the sector — and it is uniquely evaluable.** Signatories commit to 20%+ grantmaking growth or 8%+ payout for two fiscal years **and to publishing Form 990s or grant data so the commitment can be verified**. That verification mechanism is unusual and specific. I found **no evidence anyone has yet run the verification**. Signatory counts I retrieved are mutually inconsistent (35 / 40+ / 70+ / ~90 across sources), which itself suggests nobody is tracking rigorously.

5. **The merger surge is not supported by rate data.** Candid reportedly puts nonprofit merger incidence at **~1% of organizations per year** — a baseline that has not visibly moved. Merger *infrastructure* is genuinely expanding (SeaChange funds, La Piana, a new America's Promise Alliance / Monitor Deloitte M&A practice), and *intent* is elevated, but infrastructure and intent are not consummated mergers. Treat "merger wave" as [Announced]/[Anecdote], not [Documented].

6. **General operating support genuinely appears to have stepped up in the aggregate grant data — this is the strongest pro-funder finding.** GEO's 2025 study reportedly puts GOS at **38% of funding after roughly two decades near 20%**; Foundation Source data reportedly shows **40.3% of 2024 grants as GOS, up from 37.1% in 2023**. Two independent datasets pointing the same direction. Caveat: this measures *share of dollars*, not whether the median grantee's experience improved — and finding 2 says it did not.

7. **Staffing contraction is broad and measured.** Layoff *plans* more than doubled (3%→7%; 15% among disrupted orgs), hiring plans fell 52%→38%, and **51% of organizations absorbing three or more simultaneous disruption types reduced staff**. This is among the better-evidenced items in the file.

8. **Emergency capital reaching operating costs: the brief predicted a documented failure, and I found supporting but incomplete evidence.** The clearest instance is reimbursement-side, not grant-side: nearly 100 food pantries and emergency providers reportedly fronting **$2M in food and operating costs owed to them** under the Emergency Food and Shelter Program. Bridge lending demand is corroborated (SeaChange reportedly fielding 1–2 requests/week on payment delays). I did **not** find a study that systematically tests whether emergency grant capital could reach operating costs. The hypothesis is supported directionally, not proven.

9. **AI-as-cost-response is real in usage and near-absent in measured payoff.** 92% report some AI use; only **7% report major impact**; **65% describe use as reactive and individual** rather than organizational. Source is a fundraising-CRM vendor — adoption-inflating bias applies to the numerator, which makes the 7% impact figure the more trustworthy half.

10. **States raising revenue is not the same as states backfilling nonprofits, and the sources do not support conflating them.** Seven states reportedly enacted significant revenue-raisers (Illinois up to $1.8B/yr; New York second-home and corporate rate measures). These target health coverage, food assistance, and clean energy — general fiscal capacity, not nonprofit contract backfill. I found **no dollar figure for state money specifically replacing lost federal nonprofit funding in any state.**

### 2. The federal-to-foundation pivot and the operations/programs mismatch

**The mismatch is real and long-documented in principle, but I could not find a source naming it explicitly in the 2025–26 federal-cuts context.** This was a specific ask and it partially failed.

What I retrieved:
- Council on Foundations (2020): foundations commonly "give limited general operational support in addition to project-based grants," while general support is "critically needed."
- COGR comparison, research-funding-specific: **NIH applies a 50% F&A rate vs. a 10% foundation rate**; but because of differing bases, recovery lands at a similar share of total — **25.7% (NIH) vs 21.7% (foundation)**. Useful and quantified, but it describes *university research overhead*, not human-services operating support. **Do not generalize it to the sector.**
- Exponent Philanthropy and an Arkansas trade piece framed the dynamic qualitatively: without stable government funding, nonprofits "lean harder on private foundations."

**Evidence grade: [Reported] for the general principle; the specific structural-mismatch framing in the federal-cuts context is [UNVERIFIED].** The arithmetic case — that federal awards funded operations and indirect at rates foundations do not match — is highly plausible and near-certainly documented somewhere in the window, but **I did not retrieve a source that states it explicitly**. Flagging rather than asserting. One dedicated search (search 8) returned mostly off-target results.

### 3. The pivot to state/local funding — "the Great Handoff"

**Term verification — this was a specific ask and it produced a clean negative result.**

Two dedicated searches (searches 2 and 3) found the phrase in **exactly one place**: FundRobin, "2026 Nonprofit Funding Landscape Playbook," filed under the vendor's own "thought leadership" path. FundRobin is a grant-discovery product; the piece's argument (grants are getting harder to find across fifty states) maps directly onto what the product sells. My attempt to fetch the page was blocked (403), so I could not read it in full or check its citations.

Absent from results: National Council of Nonprofits, Urban Institute, Candid, CEP, GEO, Johnson Center, Chronicle of Philanthropy, Nonprofit Quarterly.

**Verdict: "The Great Handoff" is NOT an established sector term.** It is a vendor coinage with no traced uptake. If the client believes they encountered it in the sector, they most likely encountered this page. **Recommendation: do not use it as though it were common currency.** The phenomenon can be described plainly as federal-to-state pass-through devolution.

**The underlying phenomenon — [Reported], and weaker than the framing implies.**
- CBPP: **seven states enacted significant revenue-raising policies** to mitigate federal cuts from the 2025 reconciliation law.
- **Illinois: up to $1.8 billion in new annual revenue** — new levies on advertising and social media companies, plus limits on federal tax-code conformity. Illinois' budget office published a five-year federal-impact report (updated 2026-02-06).
- **New York:** new tax on high-value second homes in NYC; extension of the 7.25% top corporate income tax rate.
- **California:** proposals only in what I retrieved — advocacy groups sought budget backfill, and a billionaire wealth tax was proposed. A February 2026 bill "to shield nonprofits from federal budget cuts" was reported (Davis Vanguard); **contents and status unknown.**

**Critical caveat, stated plainly: none of these are nonprofit backfill.** They are general revenue measures aimed at health coverage, food assistance, and clean energy. **NO DATA FOUND on state dollars specifically replacing lost federal nonprofit funding, in any state.** And CBPP's framing — seven states acting, others taking "a more harmful path" — means the large majority of states did not act at all. **A "handoff" implies someone caught it; the evidence shows most states did not reach for it.**

### 10. Unrestricted and multi-year general operating support

**Funder-side data — [Documented], genuinely positive.**
- **GEO, *2025 National Study of Philanthropic Practice*: general operating support reached 38% of funding, after roughly two decades near 20%.**
  *Metadata:* **n = 765 grantmakers**; **fielded January–March 2025**; sent to all staffed U.S. foundations; some items report on operating years 2022–2024. Sponsor: Grantmakers for Effective Organizations — **membership-association bias: GEO's members self-select for "effective practice," and GEO's mission is promoting these very practices. Respondents are not representative of all foundations.**
  Same study: **74% say DEI is somewhat/very central, up from 45% in 2017**; **96% evaluate at least occasionally, up from 77% in 2017.**
- **Foundation Source data: 40.3% of 2024 grants were GOS, up from 37.1% in 2023.** Foundation Source serves primarily small and family foundations — **different universe, vendor-sourced,** but an independent corroboration of direction.

**Grantee-side confirmation — this is the critical test the brief demanded, and the answer is NO.**

**CEP *State of Nonprofits 2026*:**
- **Close to 60% of nonprofit CEOs say it has become HARDER to secure foundation grants since January 2025.**
- **Nearly half say federal grants also became harder.**
- **More than 40% report actual REDUCTIONS in foundation funding received.**
- **73% report increased demand for services.**
- **Nearly 40% ran a 2025 budget deficit, up from 22% in 2022.**

*Metadata:* **887 nonprofit leaders surveyed, 380 organizations responding, 43% response rate; fielded February 2026.** Sponsor: Center for Effective Philanthropy — independent, funder-facing, **no commercial stake in the finding; if anything CEP's audience is funders, making an unflattering-to-funders finding more credible.**

**The verdict, stated cleanly: aggregate grant dollars shifted toward general operating support, while the median grantee simultaneously experienced foundation funding as scarcer and, for 40%+, smaller.** These are compatible — flexibility improved for a subset while the overall pool tightened or concentrated — but **any narrative claiming funders "showed up" for nonprofits in this period is contradicted by grantee testimony.**

**Historical baseline — CEP, *New Attitudes, Old Practices* (2020, pre-window but directly on point):** only **41% of nonprofit leaders received multiyear GOS** in the year before COVID, and **most said it was under 25% of their foundation funding.** Methodology: **168 foundation CEOs, 105 program officers** (foundations giving ≥$5M/yr), **212 nonprofit leaders, 24 interviews.** Its central finding — a **disconnect between foundation leaders' attitudes and nonprofits' experience, and between CEOs' attitudes and their own foundations' practices** — is precisely the pattern reappearing in 2026. **The title alone is the thesis of this entire section.**

## Disconfirming evidence

The brief required active disconfirmation on three claims. All three are, to varying degrees, **overstated.**

### A. The merger surge — LARGELY DISCONFIRMED

- **Candid: only ~1% of nonprofits merge each year.** No retrieved source shows this rate moving.
- Retrieved framing repeatedly notes mergers "remain rare **despite** the strategic benefits."
- The one dataset suggesting a surge (19% recently merged / 51% likely within a year) is **untraceable and internally implausible** against the 1% baseline — discarded per methodology.
- What *is* growing is **infrastructure and intent**: SeaChange's funds, La Piana's practice, funder collaboratives, the new APA/Monitor Deloitte practice. Advisory capacity growing is what you would expect *either* from a coming wave *or* from intermediaries marketing into distress. **These are observationally equivalent, and only completed-merger counts distinguish them — which nobody published.**
- **Verdict: infrastructure up, intent up, consummated mergers NOT DEMONSTRATED.** "Merger wave" should not be asserted.

### B. Trust-based philanthropy is spreading — PARTIALLY DISCONFIRMED

- **"Many funders have returned to pre-pandemic norms"** after adopting streamlined reporting and flexible funding in 2020. **The sector has already run this experiment once and regressed** — the strongest structural argument against the spreading claim.
- **CEP *New Attitudes, Old Practices* (2020)** documented the exact pattern in its title: **attitudes shifted, practices did not.** Only **41% of nonprofits received multiyear GOS**, mostly at under 25% of their foundation funding.
- **CEP *State of Nonprofits 2026*: ~60% of nonprofit CEOs found foundation grants HARDER to secure; 40%+ received LESS.** **Grantee experience directly contradicts the adoption narrative.**
- Chronicle opinion: **"Even in an Era of Trust-Based Philanthropy, Grantees Can't Trust Funders"** — an explicit in-sector challenge.
- Selective retrenchment: some arts funders pulling back.
- Unresolved tension between reduced reporting and impact measurement — a live pressure toward re-imposing requirements.
- **Counter-evidence for the claim (fairly stated):** the GOS share rise (20%→38% per GEO; 37.1%→40.3% per Foundation Source) is real, measured, and directionally supportive. **The dollar-flexibility story holds; the relational/burden story does not.**
- **Verdict: aggregate flexibility improved; grantee-felt adoption did not. Signature counts measure enthusiasm, not practice.**

### C. Payout increases — SUBSTANTIALLY DISCONFIRMED AT SECTOR LEVEL

- **Independent-foundation payout FYE2025: unchanged, still 5%.**
- **CEP: 87% saw increased demand; only 30% increased payout — ~70% did not.** Stated reason: **boards fear eroding endowments.**
- The named increases are **a small vanguard**, several of which (Woods, Northwest Area, Marguerite Casey, TransitCenter) have **no follow-through evidence at all.**
- Level Up's own framing — *"a test"* of whether higher payouts become the norm — concedes that as of announcement, **they were not the norm.**
- **Partially countervailing:** Inside Philanthropy reports payout "increasingly tops 5%" among (probably) small/family foundations via Foundation Source. **[UNVERIFIED]** — I could not open either source to reconcile the universes.
- **Verdict: a real vanguard, a flat sector. Present both or neither.**

### D. Additional disconfirmation not requested — states "stepping in"

The devolution narrative implies states caught the handoff. Evidence retrieved: **only seven states enacted significant revenue-raisers**, aimed at health/food/energy rather than nonprofit contracts; **no state-level nonprofit backfill dollar figure exists in my results**; and **58% of surveyed Central New York nonprofits reported delays from *New York State* contracts** — states are themselves a source of cash-flow distress. **"The Great Handoff" describes a throw, not a catch.**
